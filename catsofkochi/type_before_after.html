Serif body, sans captions

The Guardian's arrangement, and the one font we now download to get it.

You said: use whatever the Guardian has proven, and change ours to fit. Two things follow, and the second reverses my earlier advice.

1. The body becomes a serif and captions become a sans — the opposite of what the site does today. 2. We now download one font, 24.5 KB, because the free-and-already-on-the-phone option turned out to be measurably worse for most of your readers.

Why I changed my mind about downloading a font

I previously said ship none. That was right about the old fonts and wrong about what replaces them, because I never checked what an Android phone actually shows. 82% of your readers are in India, overwhelmingly on Android.

For scale: one photograph on your site is many times bigger than this. The words appear immediately in the stand-in font and swap to the real one the moment it lands — so nothing waits on it.

⚠️ I gave you two wrong numbers before this one — 24.5 KB, then 43.9 KB — and I also said we could trim about 19 KB off for free. That saving does not exist. The files are already as small as they go. Please ignore it; there is nothing for anyone to go and do.

61.1 KB is what a reader actually downloads, weighed on the real files. The cause was mine: I was measuring what Google's server handed my browser, and it hands different browsers slightly different versions of the same font. The lesson is simple — weigh the file we ship, not the one we looked at.

Noticia Text, free and openly licensed. Of eight fonts I measured it was the smallest, and it is drawn for exactly this job — news text on a screen, which is what the Guardian's own font is for.

⭐ It also writes numbers evenly. Georgia sets “40” and “9.30” with letters that bob up and down — fine in a novel, wrong on a page where the fares and the ferry times are the point.

⭐ Italics are included and downloaded. I had left them out to save 13 KB; you said not to over-optimise against readability, and you were right — a memoir uses italics for dish names, boat names and emphasis, and a computer-slanted serif looks exactly like what it is.

⛔ Still not bought: a second font for the small print (your phone's own is excellent there) and a separate headline font.

⭐ The best thing this pass turned up, and it is specific to you: this font has no ₹ sign. Checked twice — it has 227 characters and the rupee is not one of them. So every rupee symbol would be drawn by a different font: one odd-looking character in the middle of every price, on a site where all the prices are in rupees.

Not worth fixing with code. In the writing, say “40 rupees” — which reads better for a visitor who may not know the symbol anyway. In a tight facts strip, ₹ is fine. And the general lesson is now a rule: always check the rupee before choosing a font for an Indian site.

The paragraph gap goes to exactly theirs — nothing about your reader makes it different. The other two stop short of theirs on purpose, and the reasons are written into the files: your reader is outside in the sun, and your pieces are long reads, not news.

That is the rule now, for everything: take what the Guardian has proven, and only depart from it when we can name something about your reader that is genuinely different. “We do it differently” is no longer a reason.
