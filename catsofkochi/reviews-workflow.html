State B. Turning one down. The reason is not a category — it is the sentence the writer receives, and that makes it design, not admin.

Why are you turning this down?

Pick one. They get the sentence underneath it.

  • It reads like an advert "Thanks for writing in. We have not put this one up — we do not publish reviews that promote a business or carry a phone number."
  • It names a member of staff "Thanks for writing. We have left this one off because it names someone who works there. Send it again without the name and we will put it up."
  • We cannot tell which place it is about "Thanks for writing. We could not work out which place this was about, so we have not put it up. Send it again and name the place?"
  • It is about something we cannot check "Thanks for writing. We have not put this one up, because it makes a claim about the place we have no way to look into."
  • It insults somebody "Thanks for writing. We have not put this up. We do not publish reviews that insult people."

Nothing is deleted. A turned-down review stays in the "Turned down" list with the reason, so a decision can be looked at again.

What this kit is, and what in it is not real

Everything a reader or the owner would see is above this band. Everything below it is commentary about the mock and is excluded from every measurement, because measuring a designer's own notes as if they were the design is the proxy error this system exists to prevent.

This feature does not exist. No code, no ticket, nothing built. These are first drawings for a reaction.

⛔ Every review, name and number on this page is invented

  • Priya M., Tom H. and jk_travels are not people and their words are written by me. No visitor has ever reviewed anything on this site.
  • 4.3, 9 reviews, "8 of 9", "3.1" and every count in the queue are invented to give the layout something to hold. They are not measurements of anything.
  • The one thing on this page that is real is what Priya's review corroborates: the board at Bastion Bungalow says 9.00 and the owner went and found it opens at 10.00. That is the single genuine posted-versus-observed record in the registry.

The walk-through this was drawn from

Priya, 34, from Bengaluru, three days in Kochi, on a phone on mobile data.

  1. 9.20 on a Tuesday, outside Bastion Bungalow. The gate board says 9.00. It is shut. Our page said 10.00 and she half-believed it.
  2. 4pm, on the bus back. She opens the page again. At the foot: "No reviews yet. Been here? Yours would be the first."
  3. She taps. She has no account. Sign-in gate, then an email and a code — under a minute, no password.
  4. The form. Four stars, "open when I arrived: no, at 9.20", three sentences, and yes to "did our page match".
  5. Nothing appears. The screen says a person reads every review first.
  6. That evening, Shemeer on his phone. Four waiting. He reads hers, sees it backs the hours record, publishes.
  7. Two days later a second traveller sees "2 reviews · a score appears once four people have been" and reads Priya's words in full.

⚠️ The gap between frames 5 and 6 is where this feature fails

If Shemeer is away, Priya's review sits for three weeks and she concludes it was binned. Nothing on the reader's side can fix that. Two things on the owner's side can, and both are in the drawing: an email on publish and on turn-down, and the age in days on every item in the queue, so a backlog is visible to the one person who can clear it.

The threshold: four reviews, within the last two years

Airbnb's number is three, and copying it would be the wrong move for three reasons.

  • Their three is bought. Only a guest who completed a paid stay can review, so faking one costs a booking. Here the entitlement is a free account, so each review is cheaper to produce and worth less as evidence. The bar should be at least as strict as theirs, not looser. One step stricter is four.
  • Four is where one voice stops deciding. At three reviews a single outlier moves the mean by up to 1.33 stars; at four, by at most 1.00, and it takes two people agreeing to shift the printed figure by a whole star. Four is the smallest count at which the number is about the place rather than about one person's morning.
  • Five or more would kill it. Twenty-six places and a small readership: set the bar at five and the score never appears anywhere, and a feature that never renders is not a feature. Four is the strictest number that is still reachable.

And it is four within two years, never a bare total. Every threshold Airbnb publishes is a count with a recency window attached — three reviews, five in four years, twelve months reassessed quarterly. A reputation that cannot decay is not a reputation. Two years and not their four, because a Fort Kochi café changes hands faster than a house does.

The honest consequence: almost every place will sit at "no score yet" for a long time, and the individual reviews will be all there is. That is the same state this site already ships for opening hours — 23 of 26 places have none — and the answer is the same one Airbnb reached: publish what people observed, withhold the average, and name the number that would make one appear.

What was cut, and why

  • Six category ratings, cut to two. Airbnb ships six for a stay (cleanliness, accuracy, check-in, communication, location, value) and — measured on the same site the same day — zero for an experience. A free-to-enter museum in a walking guide is much closer to an experience than to a booked house. Two ratings and two counted facts is what a casual reviewer will finish on a bus.
  • "How crowded was it", cut. Crowding is a time-of-day fact, and averaging it destroys the only useful thing about it — that this place is full at 11am and empty at 4pm. If it is ever wanted, it belongs beside the opening hours, not in a rating.
  • The star histogram, cut. Five bars need enough reviews for the shape to mean something. At nine it is decoration.
  • A sort control, cut for now — and one warning if it ever comes back. Airbnb's default sort is personalised to the reader from their country, language and prior searches, so two people see the same score over a different set of sentences. On a site whose whole claim is that a fact is the same for everyone, that is the one thing that could be copied by accident. Any ordering built here must be reader-independent. Newest first is the honest default.
  • Replies from venues, cut. Airbnb gives a host exactly one public reply, indented, a step smaller. It is a good shape and this is not the moment for it — it turns a reader-facing feature into a business-facing one and brings its own legal surface.

Three decisions taken here, because the screen is where they land

  • No amber anywhere in a review surface. Amber on this site means "we checked this". A review is the one thing on the page nobody here checked. There is exactly one amber underline on this whole page and it points away from a review, at the hours record it supports.
  • The separation sentence sits above the reviews, at body size, in primary ink — not as a footnote and not as grey small print. An average is a device for dissolving disagreement; this site's value is one accountable party's checked fact preserved against the crowd. They are opposite instruments and no styling reconciles them, so the page says which is which in words before it shows either.
  • "Was it open when you arrived" is a required question, and its answer is published below the threshold. It is a count of what happened, not an opinion, so it needs no crowd and no average to be true. It is also the only thing a stranger at a door can give this site that this site cannot get for itself.

Two defects this page found in patterns it copied

  • Airbnb's "Show more" is a sibling of the clamp box, so it renders whether or not anything is hidden — a review reading only "Great stay." gets one. Nothing compares scroll height to client height. Here the control starts hidden and a script at the foot reveals it only where the text really overflows. Tom H.'s one-line review has no button; Priya's does.
  • An unselected star may not be drawn in the hairline. Airbnb's empty star is their hairline grey, which is fine on a display where the number is printed alongside — and wrong on an input control, where it computes to 1.41:1 against paper and a reader in glare cannot see the thing they are meant to tap. The input stars here are outlined in secondary ink at 7.38:1; only the display stars use the hairline.

Four things that were wrong until the page was looked at

All four were invisible in the source and obvious in a screenshot at 375px. They are listed because the fix matters less than the fact that reading the CSS would never have found any of them.

  • Every question on the form had a stray hairline running out of its right side. A legend is laid out inside the fieldset's top border, so the border is interrupted for exactly the width of the question and the remainder renders as a line starting mid-sentence. Now a div with role="group", which is what the pair maps to in the accessibility tree anyway.
  • The star rows were indented against every other element. A 30px star centred in a 52px tap box starts 11px in, so the row hung to the right of the text above it. Pulled back by 11px.
  • The three tabs in the queue needed 298px and had 285. The third one ran into the card's own padding — no page-level overflow, so no overflow check would have caught it. The row wraps now, rather than scrolling, because a scroll rail hides the third option and the third option is where a turned-down review gets looked at again.
  • The breakdown had bar charts and they had to go. At "2 of 2" the bar fills its whole track, so what rendered was a solid 4px ink rule the width of the column — and 4px in this system already means the leading edge of a note. Three printed numbers, one of which is a duration, do not need a chart.

⚠️ Three things outside design that block this, and one is a lawyer question

  • Publishing someone else's words about a named business is a legal position, not a feature. Reading every review before it appears is the honest choice and it is also the one that makes the site the publisher of what it lets through. Two specific exposures: a review naming an individual, and a review alleging something illegal or unsanitary about a named business. The turn-down reasons above are drawn to catch both, but whether prior moderation helps or hurts here needs the lawyer already engaged on the commerce terms.
  • Accounts bring data-protection duties that the site does not have today. An account is an email, a display name and a history of where somebody went — under India's DPDP Act that needs an itemised notice, a retention rule and a working "delete me". The form says "deleting your account takes your reviews with it", which is a promise the build has to keep.
  • Open accounts mean the queue is the only defence. Anyone can make an account, so the second card in the queue — six five-star reviews with a phone number in two days — is not a hypothetical. The queue shows account age and review count for that reason. Nothing here stops a determined person; it makes them visible to one reader.
