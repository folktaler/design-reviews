What this is, who it is for, and what to build first

A shaping document for the forked Open Collective platform. Written 9 August 2026. For Shemeer. Nothing here is decided — the decisions are listed at the end.

This supersedes the version written earlier the same day

The assumption that changed: the earlier document assumed Aikyam would be a single fiscal host, holding money on behalf of individual fellows who have no registration of their own. It is dead.

The real model, in the Builder's words:

"aikyam and orgs like aikyam. each org will have its own razorpay account. including aikyam. money flows from donor to the orgs bank accoutn directly. we dont touch anything."

Nobody holds anybody else's money. Every organisation keeps its own payment account and its own bank account. A donor's rupee goes from her phone into that organisation's bank. Aikyam never has custody of a rupee that is not its own. There is no fiscal host here, and no platform balance.

What that costs, said plainly, because it was nearly expensive:

What the old assumption produced Where it stands now
Advice to apply for RazorpayX this week Wrong. Do not apply. Nobody needs it — see section 6. Caught before the application went in.
A recommendation to build for one host, not many Reversed. Many organisations, each holding only its own money. Costs nothing — 33 hosts already work in the running fork.
"Pay the shop directly from her platform balance" as the highest-value change There is no platform balance. The idea survives as a policy for how an organisation uses its own bank, not as a feature.
An unregistered individual as a recipient of money Cannot exist under the corrected model. She has no payment account and no bank account in an organisation's name. She is paid by an organisation, as a person that organisation supports. This is the single largest cut in this document.
A Razorpay payment provider built inside Open Collective's own code Will not merge. Under the rule below it belongs in a separate program. The payment logic moves across; the platform plumbing around it is discarded. Roughly a week's work lost, not a month's.

The rule every recommendation in here obeys

Decided by the Builder the same day:

"we should not build any new feature on opencollective base. only settings on/off etc or config levels. anything that it does not already cover can be built as a helper app only."

The reason is a measurement, not a preference: the word stripe appears in 73 files outside its own folder on the server side and 79 files on the front end. Anything built the same way becomes a merge conflict on every upstream release, forever, in a codebase nobody here wrote.

So every item in this document carries one of three labels:

Label What it means
Setting A switch Open Collective already has. Turned on or off. Minutes.
Configuration Information typed into Open Collective through its own screens or its API — an organisation, a payment method, a fundraising goal. Hours.
Helper app A separate small program running beside Open Collective, holding its own data, talking to Open Collective through its published interface. This is where all the real building happens.

Nothing in this document changes Open Collective's own code. If something genuinely could not be done any of those three ways I would say so here. Nothing hit that wall.

1. What Open Collective is actually doing, in one sentence

It is not the payment processor. It cannot be — money never passes through it.

Open Collective is the shared public account book: it records money each organisation has already received and already spent, in one place, in one format, that anyone can read.

And a sentence a stranger would understand:

It is where a few Indian organisations keep one public account book, so anyone who gave can see what it bought.

Who it is not for. Individuals with no organisation — they cannot receive money here, only be paid by an organisation. Organisations that want somebody else to hold their money — nobody does that here. Donors outside India — refused on purpose. Anyone looking for a payment gateway — Razorpay already exists and is better at it.

Is that job worth running a forked platform for? Honestly: it is a close call.

What the fork gives, already working, that would otherwise have to be built:

  • Accounts, roles and permissions across many independent organisations
  • A double-entry ledger that does not go wrong
  • Submit a bill → two people approve → mark it paid, with the bill attached and a trail of who did what
  • Public pages with a goal, a running total and the names of everyone who gave
  • An interface the helper app can write into

What it costs:

  • Four separate services to keep running, on a box that has already run out of memory once
  • No supported way to install or upgrade it — the published guidance says deployment requires being on Open Collective's own team
  • Nobody here wrote a line of it

And the uncomfortable part: under this model, the donor never sees Open Collective at all. She gives on the helper app's page. She sees Open Collective afterwards, as a read-only record. So the fork is being used as a public ledger and an internal approval tool — a fraction of what it is.

Recommend keeping it, running it completely stock, with a kill test at 90 days — needs your yes. It is already standing, the config-only rule keeps it cheap, and the helper app has to be built either way and survives whichever way this goes. But name the test now, in advance:

By day 90 from first public rupee: at least two organisations other than Aikyam have a live public page, and at least one bill has been published on a page next to the contributions that paid for it. If either is false, the fork is oversized and should be replaced by a small page the house owns and can explain.

One thing flips this immediately: if Open Collective cannot be run on existing hardware without paying for more. A monthly bill for a noticeboard used by two organisations is not defensible. That is a check for architect, not a decision for me: can this stack run on the current box, and what does it cost if not.

2. Three storyboards

Everyone in this section is invented. Priya, Rahitha, Nusrat and Chalanam Foundation are illustrative characters written to test the product, not real people or real organisations, and nothing said about them — including any registration or tax status — describes anybody actual. Any resemblance to a real organisation of a similar name is coincidence.

Not features. Three real people, moment by moment.

A — Priya gives ₹500

Priya, 34, works in Bengaluru. 9pm on a Tuesday. She has never heard of Aikyam.

# The moment
1 A friend sends her a WhatsApp link. Just: "read this."
2 She opens it on her phone, on the bus. It is a profile of Rahitha, who teaches chenda to fourteen girls in Thrissur — an instrument girls were not taught.
3 She finishes reading and feels something. And there is nothing to do. She closes the tab.
4 (the frame that does not exist yet) At the bottom: "Aikyam is raising ₹40,000 so Rahitha's students have six chenda of their own. Eleven people have given ₹23,400."
5 She taps. One screen. Three amounts: ₹500, ₹1,000, ₹2,500. She picks ₹500.
6 Name, email, PAN. She types the PAN because the line under it says "so we can send you an 80G tax receipt." There is a small link: give without a receipt. She ignores it.
7 UPI. GPay opens. Thumbprint. Nine seconds. The money is now in Aikyam's own bank account. Nothing and nobody else touched it.
8 The page has changed. Her name is on it. ₹23,900. The receipt is in her inbox before she gets off the bus.
9 Three weeks later, one email. Not a newsletter. A photograph of six chenda in a rented room, one line — "they arrived Tuesday, first class Thursday" — and a link.
10 The link opens a page showing ₹38,200 paid to Peruvanam Music House on 4 September, the bill attached, and above it the ₹23,900 from twelve people that paid for it. Her ₹500 is one of the lines.
11 She forwards it to the friend who sent her the link. "Look."

The gap is between 3 and 4. Everything Aikyam publishes today ends at frame 3. The reason to exist is 9 to 11. Any organisation can build frames 5 to 8 in an afternoon with Razorpay alone. Almost nobody builds 10.

B — Rahitha gets paid

Rahitha, 29, teaches chenda in Thrissur. No company, no registration, no way to issue a receipt.

# The moment
1 She rents a room for ₹2,000 a month and pays for it herself. She has never asked anyone for money — partly because she does not know how, partly because asking feels like begging.
2 Aikyam interviews her and publishes her profile. (This already happens.)
3 Someone from Aikyam says: we will raise it for you. You do not register anything, you do not open anything. We raise it, we hold it in our account, and we pay for what you need.
4 She writes three lines and a number. ₹40,000, six chenda.
5 It goes live. She did not have to ask a single person directly.
6 Money arrives from strangers. She can watch it happen on a page.
7 She goes to the instrument shop in Peruvanam. This is where it works or does not.
8 (the wrong way) She pays ₹38,200 of her own money, photographs the bill, uploads it, and waits eleven days for a reimbursement. She has just lent money she did not have. She will not do it again, and she will tell the others.
9 (the right way) She sends the shop's quotation on WhatsApp. Two people at Aikyam approve it on their phones. Aikyam's accountant pays the shop by UPI from Aikyam's own bank account that afternoon. Rahitha's own money is never involved.
10 The payment is recorded on the public page with the shop's name, the date and the bill.
11 She tells another person Aikyam profiled: "do it, it actually pays."

Note what changed from the old plan. Frame 9 is not a platform feature any more. It is a bank transfer a human makes, and a record a human enters. The platform's only job in this storyboard is frame 10 — putting it in public. That is a much smaller claim than the old document made, and it is the honest one.

Note also what Rahitha is not. She has no account, no balance and no page of her own. She is a person Aikyam pays. Everything about her that the earlier document called fiscal hosting is gone.

C — Nusrat brings the second organisation

Nusrat, 41, runs Chalanam Foundation in Kozhikode. Nine staff. She holds FCRA registration, which Aikyam does not. She does her own books with a part-time CA.

# The moment
1 She has known Aikyam for two years. Somebody shows her Aikyam's public page on a phone at a meeting. Her first thought is not admiration, it is how much work is that.
2 She is told: your money stays in your bank, your Razorpay account, your books. Nobody sees your balance. What you publish is what you choose to publish.
3 This is the sentence the whole product depends on. If she does not believe it in ten seconds, there is no second organisation and no platform.
4 She sets up Chalanam's own Razorpay account. KYC takes eleven days. Nothing else can start until it is done.
5 Someone from Aikyam fills in Chalanam's details: its 80G number, its bank, its payment keys. Half an hour, following a checklist. No code.
6 Chalanam's giving page goes live. The first ₹1,000 lands in Chalanam's own bank account forty minutes later.
7 Because Chalanam has FCRA and Aikyam does not, she expects to take foreign money. She is told no — not this year, not through this. Foreign money must land in one specific designated bank account and cannot be mixed with anything. She takes it the way she always has, outside this, and records it by hand.
8 31 May. Her CA asks for every donor's name, PAN and address for the annual filing. She downloads one file. It takes four minutes.
9 (the version where this went wrong) If PAN had not been asked at frame 6, this file cannot be produced. Not "is hard" — cannot. The donors are gone, the deadline is fixed, and the penalty is ₹200 a day plus a fine.
10 Her certificates go out to her donors. Nobody at Aikyam ever saw a single PAN — Chalanam's donor details never left Chalanam's own file.
11 She tells a third organisation about it.

Frames 8 to 10 are why the compliance work sits in release one and not later. And frame 3 is why nothing in this product may ever require an organisation to hand over control of its money.

3. The eleven-star ladder

The moment: Priya finding out what her ₹500 bought. Frames A9 to A11.

Why this moment. Giving is a commodity — every Indian platform has a UPI button and has had one for a decade; we will never be better at it, only adequate. Being paid now happens in a bank, outside the software entirely. What is left, and what nothing else in the country does well, is the loop closing: a stranger seeing the exact thing her money became. It is also the only honest answer to "what is Open Collective for."

Stars What happens
3 An annual report PDF arrives in December. She does not open it.
4 A quarterly newsletter with photographs. She skims it.
5 Three weeks later, one email: a photo of six chenda in a rented room, "they arrived Tuesday."
6 The email links to a public page where the ₹38,200 bill sits directly above the ₹23,900 from twelve people that paid it — and one of those lines is hers. She can show it to someone.
7 The page has already done the arithmetic for her, in a sentence she did not have to assemble: "Your ₹500 was part of the ₹23,900 that bought six chenda from Peruvanam Music House on 4 September." The bill is attached to that sentence.
8 She chose this. Before she gave, the page said "₹40,000 for six chenda" — she was not giving to an organisation, she was giving to a thing. Her money had an address from the moment it left her.
9 Twenty seconds of phone video from the first class, on the same page as the ledger line.
10 A year on, one page shows every rupee she has given across three organisations and what each became. She never asked for it and never signed up for it.
11 Rahitha's fourteen students play at Priya's cousin's wedding in Bengaluru, and the invitation card says the drums were bought by strangers who read a story on a bus.

Six is the build. Seven is nearly free once six exists — it is a sentence, not a system: the helper app already knows which contributions and which bill it linked, so it can write the sentence into the email. Together they are the release.

Eight is a configuration choice, not a build: raise money against a named goal rather than into a general pot. Costs nothing and doubles the value of six.

Nine, ten and eleven are why you would keep going.

What this ladder rules out. Notice that no rung is made better by Open Collective processing a payment, holding a balance, or running a marketplace. Every rung above 5 is about the record being legible to a stranger. That is the product. Everything else in the box is scenery.

4. What to cut

The constraint, so this is a real choice and not a shopping list: one build effort, eight weeks, two organisations live, before anything is public. Change the constraint if it is wrong, but do not remove it — the last time a list was offered without one, nineteen of twenty came back, which is not an answer.

Under the config-only rule, "cut" now means one of three specific things, and it is worth being precise about which:

  • Switch it off — it exists in the box and costs nothing but confusion. Turn it off.
  • Do not build the helper for it — this is where real weeks are saved.
  • Do not go and get the approval — this is where real calendar is saved, which is scarcer.
Cut Which kind Why — the frame test
Fiscal hosting: host applications, host fees, hosted-collective onboarding, transfers between organisations Switch off Dead by definition. Nobody holds anybody's money. Every organisation hosts only itself. Frame C2 — this is the promise the second organisation joins for.
Everything that pays money out: payout rails, Wise, PayPal payouts, virtual cards, batch payments Switch off Money leaves from an organisation's own bank, by a human, on net banking. Frame B9. The platform records it afterwards.
Any platform-level payment account, including RazorpayX Do not get the approval Nobody needs one. Section 6 answers this in full. This is the single largest calendar saving in the document.
Stripe, PayPal and card payments generally Switch off Wrong country, wrong rail, and card payments are the exact route by which foreign money arrives. Verified: an INR organisation with no Stripe account works — created, took money, produced a correct rupee ledger entry.
Multi-currency and exchange rates Switch off One country, one currency. Every amount is rupees.
Events and ticketing Do not build No frame. A ticket is a sale, not a donation — different tax treatment, seat inventory, refunds. Until an organisation asks twice, a workshop is a giving option called "workshop seat" and an email sent by hand.
Tiers with rewards and benefits Do not build A goal is load-bearing (frame A4, rung 8) — keep it, it is configuration. A reward means fulfilment, which means a human posting objects to strangers. Cut.
Projects — ring-fenced sub-budgets Do not build No frame. A goal does the job of frame A4 without a second layer of accounts.
A directory of participating organisations Switch off for now With two organisations, a directory is a list of two. It costs nothing to leave off and nothing to turn on later. Aikyam's own site is already the better front door.
Discussion threads and conversations Switch off The forum already exists. Two places to talk means neither is used.
Bank reconciliation and automatic bank feeds Do not build The providers do not cover Indian banks, so the automatic half cannot work. At two organisations and a few hundred transactions a year, a file the CA opens in Excel is the correct answer, not a compromise.
Recurring monthly giving Below the line, see section 6 Deliberately switched off in the fork today, and it needs an external approval per organisation. First thing after the release line.
The reimbursement workflow as the default way to spend Do not build; make it policy Frame B8. Rahitha does not have ₹38,200 to lend for eleven days. That is a rich-country assumption. The fix is not software — it is Aikyam paying the shop directly from its own account. Free, and it is the highest-value change in this document.

Where the release line sits, and why there

Above the line is: a person can give, the organisation gets a rupee it can bank, the donor gets a receipt that is legal, the tax filing is possible, the money that goes out is visible, and the loop closes with an email. Below the line is everything that is either an optimisation or a second organisation's special case.

Item 13 is the one I would defend hardest against being pushed below. Without it the release is a donate button with extra steps, and it has no reason to exist. Note honestly that item 13 ships silent — on launch day nothing has been spent yet, so the first of those emails goes out weeks later. That is expected, not a fault.

What someone can do the day it ships

  • Priya can read a story, tap once, give ₹500 by UPI in under a minute from a bus, get an 80G receipt before she gets off, and see her own name on a public page.
  • Weeks later she gets one email showing the exact thing her money bought, with the bill.
  • Rahitha can say what she needs, watch it fill, and have the shop paid directly — without ever spending her own money first — and see it recorded in public afterwards.
  • Aikyam can see every rupee in one place, requires two people for anything going out, and hands the CA one file on 31 May.
  • Nusrat can put Chalanam live in half an hour without giving up control of a single rupee.

What it cannot

  • No monthly giving. Every gift is one-off. This is the biggest cost of the line and the first thing after it.
  • No foreign money at all — for any organisation, including one that holds FCRA. On purpose.
  • No unregistered individual receives money. They are paid by an organisation.
  • No tickets, no events, no rewards, no corporate portal, no bank feed.
  • The account book is a record, not a bank balance. See the next section — it is the main way this fails.

The way this fails, which is invisible in a feature list

Money never passes through the software, so nothing forces the public account book to match the bank. Contributions are safe: they are written in automatically the moment a payment succeeds. Money going out is not — a human types it in after making a bank transfer, and humans stop typing.

Three months in, the page shows ₹23,900 raised and nothing spent, because two payments were made from net banking on a Sunday and nobody recorded them. A public account book that is quietly wrong is worse than none, and it damages exactly the trust the whole thing is for.

There is no clever fix. There is a small one: one person named as the owner of that record, and a monthly check that the two totals agree. Say who, in writing, before launch. Item 18 automates the nagging later.

6. External approvals: what to start this week, and by whom

The RazorpayX question, answered plainly

RazorpayX is not needed by anyone. Do not apply. Cancel the application.

RazorpayX is a business banking and bulk-payouts product. It answers the question "how do I pay many people automatically from one pot of money." Under the corrected model that question is never asked:

  • Aikyam pays only its own staff, its own vendors, and people like Rahitha whom it chooses to support — from its own bank account, a handful of times a month, by net banking or plain UPI.
  • Every other organisation pays its own people from its own bank account. Aikyam has no ability to pay them and no business doing so.
  • There is deliberately no platform-wide payment account of any kind. The Razorpay work already built stores credentials per organisation precisely so that a platform-wide key can never exist. That decision was right and it stands.

If, in a year, one organisation is paying sixty people a month by hand and hates it, that organisation applies for its own RazorpayX in its own name. Not Aikyam, not the platform.

Cost of my being wrong here: if a payout product turns out to be needed later, the application takes the same few weeks then as it would now, and nothing above the release line was waiting on it. Cost of applying anyway: an unnecessary banking relationship, KYC on Aikyam's accounts, and a product sitting there inviting somebody to use it in a way the model forbids.

The third column is the point. A page that promises an 80G receipt an organisation cannot issue is worse than a page that promises nothing.

How PAN capture works

  • Where it happens: the helper app's giving screen, at the moment of payment — never afterwards.
  • Required by default, with a small visible link: give without a receipt. Taking that link records the gift as "no receipt, not reportable" and skips the field. My reasoning for required-with-an-escape rather than plain required: an optional field is an empty field, and the penalty falls on the organisation. But making it hard to give at 9pm on a bus is how frame A6 dies. This is the smallest version of both.
  • What is stored: name, PAN, address, amount, date, and how it was paid. Those are exactly the columns the annual return asks for. Collecting them in any other shape means transcribing later.
  • PANs are stored in the helper app, per organisation, and never written into Open Collective. The account book is public by design. A donor's PAN and home address must not be in it, and one organisation must never be able to read another's donor list. Frame C10.
  • The receipt goes out immediately, carrying that organisation's own registration number.
  • The file the CA opens on 31 May is generated from those same records, per organisation, by whoever runs that organisation — not by Aikyam on their behalf.

How foreign money is blocked

Three layers, because one is not enough and each is cheap.

Layer Where What it stops
International payments left switched off on each organisation's Razorpay account Setting, in Razorpay Every foreign card. This is the strong one, and it is already the default.
A residency declaration on the giving screen, and a plain line on the page saying we can only accept money from within India Helper app An NRI using an Indian-looking payment route. Not watertight, but it establishes intent and it deters.
Anything that comes back flagged as international is refused and refunded, not banked Helper app Whatever gets past the first two.

The rule for release one: every organisation refuses foreign money, including one that holds FCRA. That is deliberate and it needs saying, because it will look wrong to Nusrat. Foreign contributions must land in one specific designated bank account and be kept completely separate from everything else — that is a second bank account, a second payment account and a second set of books, for a single organisation. It is real work and it belongs below the line. Until then, an FCRA-holding organisation takes foreign money the way it does today, outside this, and records it as money that arrived elsewhere.

Cost of my being wrong: Chalanam loses some foreign giving for a year and is mildly annoyed. Cost of the reverse error: money in the wrong account, ₹1,00,000 or 30% of the amount, and a criminal matter. Not a close call.

8. The decisions that are yours

Seven. Each with a recommendation and what it costs if I am wrong.

1. Keep the forked platform, or replace it with something smaller? Recommend: keep it, run it completely stock, and write the 90-day kill test from section 1 down now — two other organisations live, one bill published against the contributions that paid it. Cost of being wrong: if it should have been dropped, three months of running a large thing for a small job, and the answer arrives cheaply because the helper app survives either way. If it is dropped too early, the account book, roles and approval trail get rebuilt from nothing — a month. One thing flips this immediately: if architect finds it cannot run on existing hardware. Then it costs money every month, and a paid noticeboard for two organisations is not defensible.

2. Can an unregistered individual ever receive money through this? Recommend: no. Never. She is paid by an organisation from that organisation's own bank. Cost of being wrong: this is the largest cut in the document and the one that follows directly from your own model — an individual has no Razorpay account and no bank account in an organisation's name, so there is nothing to flow into. If you meant something different, say so now, because it brings back everything section 4 removed and triples the legal work.

3. RazorpayX — apply, or cancel? Recommend: cancel. Nobody needs it. Section 6 gives the reasoning in full. Cost of being wrong: none today. If a payout product is needed in a year, it takes the same weeks then, and nothing above the release line waits on it.

4. UPI Autopay — apply this week? Recommend: yes, Aikyam only, on Aikyam's own account. Cost of being wrong: an unused approval. Cost of not applying: monthly giving, the first thing across the release line, starts several weeks later than it needed to.

5. PAN required at checkout, or optional? Recommend: required, with a small visible "give without a receipt" link. Cost of being wrong: if it is too much friction, some ₹500 gifts are lost at 9pm on a bus — and you will see that in the numbers within a fortnight and can soften it. The reverse error cannot be softened: no PAN in September means no filing in May, a daily penalty, and sixty donors who cannot be asked again. Wildly lopsided. Build the field.

6. Foreign money refused for every organisation in release one, including one holding FCRA? Recommend: yes, all of them. Cost of being wrong: one organisation loses some foreign giving for a year. The reverse error is criminal, so this is not a close call.

7. How many organisations before anything is public? Recommend: two — Aikyam and one other, both live, before the first public announcement. Cost of being wrong: launching with one proves nothing; the entire model rests on frame C3 being believable to a second organisation, and you only find out by trying it. Waiting for three delays everything behind somebody else's KYC. Two is the smallest number that tests the claim.

9. The sentence to keep testing

It is where a few Indian organisations keep one public account book, so anyone who gave can see what it bought.

Every time something is proposed, ask whether that sentence survives it. It survives goals, bills, receipts and a second organisation. It does not survive a marketplace, a payment product, a directory, a ticketing system, or holding anybody else's money.

And when the sentence stops being true, it is not the sentence that is wrong.
