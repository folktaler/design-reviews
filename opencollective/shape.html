What this is, who it is for, and what to build first

A shaping document. First written 9 August 2026. This is the third version. For Shemeer.

Read the correction below before anything else. Two earlier versions of this page have been read by people, and both of them argued for a product that is no longer being built.

⚠️ Correction, and it is most of the document

Version 1 said drop Open Collective, because a donor on a private collective would see nothing. That was read out of the code and was wrong.

Version 2 corrected it by running the fork: a donor keeps her own history even when the recipient is private. It then recommended private collectives, and — because a private collective serves an anonymous visitor nothing at all — it put the entire donor experience into a separate helper app of ours: sixteen items, a giving page, sign-in, her account, her history, her receipts, updates, the 80G certificate, the tax file.

Version 3, this one. That was priced, and private collectives turned out to cost roughly eleven extra things to build. The Builder chose public collectives instead, and the helper app collapsed with the premise that required it.

What is left to build is configuration, a few lines in one receipt template, and an export preset. That is the whole thing.

What this correction cost, and what it bought

Cost: four design pages built around the helper app, and one shaping round. They are not deleted — each one is re-marked and most of the thinking in them survives as a specification for configuring Open Collective rather than as a thing to build. See design-system/index.html.

Bought: the largest limitation in version 2 is gone. That document said, in bold, "She cannot show anyone what her money bought." On a public collective she can. The sharing loop is back, and it is the single most valuable thing this reversal returned.

The lesson worth keeping, because it has now happened twice in different directions: version 1 was wrong because it read code instead of running it; version 2 was wrong because it designed around a constraint before anyone had priced removing the constraint. Price the premise before you build on it.

1. What this is, in one sentence

Give to one of these Indian organisations, and you are shown the shop's bill for what your money actually bought.

Who it is not for. Individuals who want to be paid. Donors outside India. Anyone who wants a payment gateway. Anyone who wants their organisation's numbers kept private — that is now the loudest thing this product cannot offer, and section 6 is about it.

Three things nobody has established, and each one changes the product

⛔ Do not build past these. They are not technical details; each is a different product.

1. Whose Razorpay account receives the rupees. If the money lands in Aikyam's own Razorpay and Aikyam's own bank, then every sentence written for this product so far is still true. If it lands in a platform account and is passed on, then we hold other people's money, the copy is false, and the legal shape of the whole thing changed without anyone saying so out loud.

2. Whether one fiscal host holds many organisations, or each organisation is its own host. Version 2 rejected fiscal hosting outright — one body holding and disbursing money for many. The 80G number is now "configured on the fiscal host", which reads as though each organisation is its own. If it is not, fiscal hosting came back in through a settings screen, and that is the biggest decision in this document being taken by accident.

3. Whether a donor's name appears in public next to her amount. A public collective lists who contributed. Priya gave ₹500 from a bus and was never asked whether she wanted her name on a public web page. Recommendation: her name is not shown unless she chooses it. She is asked once, plainly, at the moment she gives — not buried in a profile setting she will never open.

Two frames dropped and one was raised.

The forwarding frame went from 3 to 5, and it is the whole of what the reversal bought. Her friend now lands on the actual thing — the bill, the ledger, the story — not on a locked door.

The frame that fell is the arithmetic sentence. Version 2 had a page that said "Your ₹500 was part of the ₹23,900 that bought six chenda on 4 September." An update is the same text for everybody, so the word "your" is gone. That is the real cost of deleting the helper app and it should be paid knowingly. It is section 7's only open decision.

The PAN frame fell too, and that one is cheap to fix: a tax identifier typed into a structured address form will be labelled as an address line, and a field labelled wrongly collects wrong data — lower case, spaces, the wrong line. It needs its own label, its own one-line explanation of why it is being asked, and a check of the format (five letters, four digits, one letter).

Version 2's single best sentence to a cautious organisation was "nothing is public — only the people who gave can look." That sentence is now false and there is no replacement for it. Nusrat's sharpest objection is the one thing the new model answers worst.

It is not fatal, and here is the honest version to say to her instead: what is public is the money — the balance, what came in, what went out, and the bill for it. What is not public is who gave, if they would rather not be named. If an organisation cannot live with its spending being readable, this product is not for it, and that is better said in the first conversation than the fourth.

But it should be said out loud that this narrows who will join. Two organisations before anything is announced is still the constraint, and the second one is now harder to win, not easier.

The floor rose and the ceiling fell. Three, four and six are now free or nearly free — that is much better than version 2, where every rung above five was a page we had to write. But seven and eight both went from "we build it" to "nobody does it", because the app that would have done them is gone.

  • Seven is the word "your". Buying it back means a page of ours, which means a helper app, which is the thing that was just deleted. Do not buy it back for launch. Revisit it only if the kill test in section 8 fails on click-through — and by then it is one page, not an app.
  • Eight is a person remembering. Item 15 (a 60-day nudge) is the cheap 80% of it and it sits below the line, correctly. If Devi's frame is failing at month two, move it up.

7. The decisions that are yours

Most of version 2's eight are now settled by the reversal. Four are left, and the first two are real.

1. Updates that carry a bill: public, or contributors only? Recommend: public. The collective's page, its balance, its expenses and the bill are already public, so a contributors-only update gates nothing that is not visible one click away — while costing the forward, which is the only growth this product has. Keep contributors-only for the intimate material: photographs of children, video, names, the voice note. That split is the recommendation: the money is public, the people are not. Cost of being wrong: "you can see this because you gave" is a genuine reason to give, and making the bill public spends it. Reversible per update, at any time.

2. Do we accept that seven and eight are not built? Recommend: yes, for launch. Say it out loud now rather than discovering in month three that the product is a well-kept ledger with a newsletter. Cost of being wrong: this is the difference between a product and a page. If nobody clicks, the diagnosis will be ambiguous — was it the missing "your", or was it the whole idea? Section 8 is written to tell those two apart.

3. Is her name shown in public next to her amount? Recommend: no, unless she chooses it, asked once at the moment she gives. A default that publishes a stranger's name because she gave ₹500 from a bus is a privacy event she did not agree to. Cost of being wrong: almost none. Named giving can be offered later; a name published once cannot be taken back.

4. PAN from her profile rather than at checkout, accepting that some donors are never filed. Recommend: yes, unchanged from version 2, with the certificate as the forcing line and a plain sentence telling her what she loses by not adding it. Cost of being wrong: every donor who never returns before 31 May loses her deduction. Visible in the first year; if the hole is large, ask at checkout after all.

Settled, and not up for discussion

Public collectives. No helper app. INR only. Foreign money refused by every organisation, permanently. No individual ever receives money. Two organisations live before anything is announced. A monthly check of each organisation's book against its bank statement. No feature is added to Open Collective's code, with exactly one exception — the Razorpay provider — and a second exception needs the Builder's explicit yes.

8. How this fails, and the kill test

One: nobody writes the update. Money never fails to record itself; writing does. There is no mechanism behind it any more — only Devi's Tuesday. The mitigation is not clever: one named person owns it, and a monthly check. Write the name down before launch.

Two: it turns out nobody wanted to know. The public page proved the door is open. It proves nothing about whether anyone walks through it.

By 90 days after the first "what your money bought" update goes out:

  1. At least half the contributors who received one opened it and clicked through.
  2. At least one donor came back a second time without being emailed first.
  3. Every month in that period had at least one expense recorded with its bill attached, and one update written about it.
  4. At least one forwarded link produced a contribution from somebody who had never given before.

Test 4 is new, and it is the one the reversal bought. If it passes, public collectives paid for themselves. If tests 1 and 2 fail while 3 passes, the missing piece is the word "your" — that is when to reconsider building one page. If test 3 fails, the problem is Devi's Tuesday, and no donor-facing work will fix it.

9. Two things that are outstanding no matter what is decided

  1. Five secrets are published in Open Collective's public repository. They must be replaced before anything faces the internet. This is not a launch task; it is a gate.
  2. Nobody has ever production-built the frontend. Everything above assumes it builds. Until somebody has done it once, that is an assumption, not a fact.

10. The sentence to keep testing

Give to one of these Indian organisations, and you are shown the shop's bill for what your money actually bought.

It survives receipts, updates, a second organisation, monthly giving and a public page. It does not survive a directory, a marketplace, holding anybody else's money, or an organisation that will not let its spending be read.

And when the sentence stops being true, it is not the sentence that is wrong.
